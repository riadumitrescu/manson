MARIA DUMITRESCU · DESIGN INTERN TAKEHOME

Two screens. Two redesigns.

I spent about 30 minutes inside Mason before I drew anything, because I wanted to see what actually felt clumsy in use, not just unpolished in screenshots. Two screens stood out: the Ask Mason home, where every new user forms their first impression, and the Invoice Review output, where the citation feature actually pays off the marketing promise. I redesigned those two, and noted five other things while I was in there.

One caveat: I matched Mason's look as best I could from the live product, but I don't know the exact fonts or tokens the team uses, so some pieces probably look a little off. I'd align with the real design system before shipping any of this.

★ Beyond the brief

Five things I'd add to the known-issues list.

The brief flags a few known issues like the cluttered toolbar and file sidebar, and I tackle those in the two redesigns below. But while using the app, five other things kept catching my eye that don't seem to be on the list yet.

01 · POSITIONING
The home contradicts the pitch.
  • Mason's marketing says "not just a chatbot," but the home is a centered chat box with "What can I help with today?". Same shape as every other LLM wrapper.
  • A new user has about 30 seconds before they form an opinion. They see a chatbox, file Mason as another GPT wrapper, and never find the citation feature that's supposed to set it apart.
  • My fix. Part 1 opens with a real workbook and one flagged row pulsing red, so verification is happening on screen before they type a word. Chat is still there, just demoted below the workbook.
02 · CLUTTER
Empty drafts pile up in history.
  • Recent Activity has two rows named Untitled chat, and History has two more named Untitled · Document Agent. They're threads I opened but never typed in. Mason auto-names threads with content, but empty ones still get a permanent slot.
  • Every time I want to find what I actually worked on, I scan past the abandoned starts. Small thing, but it undercuts the auditability story Mason is built around.
  • My fix. Hide empty threads from Recent, or tuck them in a collapsed "Drafts" group. Day 30 of my Part 1 redesign also promotes artifacts (workbooks, projects) above raw chat threads, since what I built is more useful to find than what I asked.
03 · GATING
Locked workflows greet the new user.
  • Three of the seven workflows in the sidebar are grayed out with orange REQUEST pills, sitting right next to the active ones. They're in the user's eyeline every time.
  • The day-one signal is "you don't have access to most of this," which is the opposite of onboarding. Either users notice and feel boxed out, or they don't notice at all and the locked items are dead weight.
  • My fix. Keep active workflows in the agent card. Move locked ones behind a "Browse all workflows" link, so they're discoverable on intent but out of the default visual load.
04 · MYSTERY CONTROL
A toggle next to "Files." No label.
  • The Files row has a small iOS-style toggle with no label and no tooltip when I hovered. I genuinely couldn't tell what it does.
  • Best guess: it controls whether all project files are loaded into the agent's context. The wrong setting means the agent quietly ignores docs you uploaded, so it's load-bearing, and hiding it in an unlabeled nav toggle is a rough spot.
  • My fix. Move it into the agent card with a real label like "Context: All project files / Selected files / None." State controls belong with the agent they affect, not the nav.
05 · CONTINUITY
Two chat inputs, one agent.
  • Ask Mason has a big central chat input. Smartsheets has a separate one docked in the right rail. Switching between them doesn't carry the conversation across.
  • This splits the mental model: "where do I tell Mason to do something" depends on which screen I'm on. Ask for a workbook in Ask Mason, then open Smartsheets to view it, and the right-rail agent doesn't know about that thread.
  • My fix. One persistent chat that follows the user across screens. The right rail becomes inspector-only, the way it works in my Part 2 below. The agent stays the same; only the artifact changes.

PART 1 · RATIONALE
Show, don't tell.
  • No wizard, no tour. The home leads with a live demo: one row of a real workbook pulses red, and clicking any row pops the citation. Users feel the citation feature before clicking a single CTA.
  • Chrome fades as the user progresses. First visit has a welcome line and progress dots. After the sample, those collapse into a Linear-style corner widget. By day 30 there's no chrome at all, just the workspace with real deals. Onboarding shouldn't be a mode you exit, just something that fades.
  • The sidebar evolves too. History goes from empty, to one sample thread, to three real ones. The progression isn't only in the hero, it's reinforced by the chrome filling in as the user does work.

WHY
The citation feature is what sets Mason apart, and the marketing is built around it. It should be the first thing new users see, not something they dig for. A working demo in the hero means they get the differentiator on first impression, instead of forming a "just another chatbot" opinion that's hard to undo.
02
The sidebar
BEFORE
Three different arrow styles. Locked workflows with REQUEST pills next to active ones. A toggle on the Files row with no label.
AFTER
One arrow style (the dropdown chevron on the agent picker). Locked workflows behind a "Browse all" link. The mystery toggle moved into the agent card with a real label.
WHY
The original had three arrow shapes (chevron-right, arrow-right, expand-chevron) doing different jobs in the same space, plus an unlabeled toggle on Files and grayed-out workflows advertising what the user couldn't access. The redesign uses one arrow style (the dropdown chevron on the agent picker) and moves the gating and mystery toggle out of the default view.
03
The agent card

Six pieces of chrome: AGENT label, "switch" link, black avatar, Context with a chevron, WORKFLOWS label, "Browse" with another arrow.

One clickable card. One chevron. Soft gray avatar. Context as an inline value. Workflows as a quiet list.
WHY
The original card had five competing bits: an AGENT label, a "switch" link, a heavy black avatar, a Context arrow, a WORKFLOWS label, and a "Browse" link with another arrow. The redesign turns the whole card into one clickable button with one chevron. The avatar lightens because it doesn't need to dominate, and the labels go away because a card laid out this way already says "this is the agent and these are its workflows."

05
Three states, one home
V1
First visit. Live demo. Welcome chip and progress dots. Lots of onboarding chrome.
V2
After the sample. Hero shifts to "what next?". Chrome shrinks to a corner widget.
V3
Day 30. No chrome at all. Active deals lead the page.

The trap with onboarding is designing it as a separate mode the user has to exit, which forces a hard cutoff between "new" and "real" users and a decision about when to flip the switch. Making the home itself the onboarding, with chrome that fades as the user does work, removes the cutoff. The same screen just reads as more or less helpful depending on where they are.

PART 2 · RATIONALE
Pin the inspector. Verify at speed.
  • The inspector stays open. The right panel is a stable three-stack: summary on top, citation in the middle, source PDF underneath. Clicking a different cell scrolls the panels instead of swapping them. No "Back to Chat" mode switch, no losing your place between cells.
  • Status next to the title. Verified, partial, and discrepancy counts plus a tri-color bar live right next to the workbook title. That's the first thing an analyst wants to skim: how much is good, how much do I have to look at.
  • Toolbar from 22 controls to 6. Workflow output is read-only and the user is inspecting, not authoring fonts. The full Excel toolbar belongs in standalone Smartsheets. What's left is the actions analysts actually use.
  • Arrow keys + "Disagree." Once an analyst trusts the citations, they want to approve at speed. Arrow keys step through cells, Enter opens the source. And when Mason gets a citation wrong, "Disagree" is a one-click way to push back instead of living with it.
PART 2 · WHAT CHANGED

Five components, before and after.

01
The toolbar
BEFORE
22 buttons. Font picker, size, bold, italic, underline, strikethrough, fill color, alignment, borders, format painter, lock cell, and more.
AFTER
6 buttons. Undo, Redo, Format dropdown, Insert, Import, Download.
WHY
Workflow output is a read-only artifact for inspecting and verifying, not a blank spreadsheet to edit. Most of the original 22 buttons (font, size, bold, italic, fill, borders, format painter, lock cell) do nothing useful here, just a wall of icons to scan past. Stripping it down makes the workbook feel like the document it actually is.
02
The status bar
Workbook 1 ● SYNCED
31 verified · 10 partial · 1 discrepancy
BEFORE
Nothing. You'd scroll the whole sheet to find a flagged row.
AFTER
A tri-color progress bar with verified, partial, and discrepancy counts. Total dollars due. Right next to the workbook title.
WHY
When an analyst opens an invoice review, they want to know "how much is verified, how many partial, how many flagged." That's the first frame of the screen, and it should be readable in about a second. The progress bar plus three counts gives them the whole status in one glance, right where their eye lands first.
03
The persistent inspector
BEFORE
A tiny "Back to Chat" inspector that swaps out when you click a different cell. Mode switch.
AFTER
A stable three-stack on the right. Summary on top, citation in the middle, source PDF docked under it. Click a different cell, the panels scroll. Nothing replaces.
WHY
Inspecting a cell shouldn't be a mode you enter and exit, because the whole point is bouncing between cells quickly. The original inspector swapped out every time you clicked a cell, breaking your place every few seconds. A persistent panel that just updates as you click lets you stay in the inspecting flow without losing context, which matters across 42 line items.
04
"Disagree with this match"

BEFORE
No way to tell Mason a citation was wrong. The user just lived with it.
AFTER
A small button on every citation card. One click captures the disagreement.
WHY
When Mason gets a citation wrong today, there's no way for the user to flag it. The user has no agency, and Mason gets no corrective signal. A one-click "Disagree" button on every citation card solves both: the user can push back without a support ticket, and the team gets training data from the people best positioned to label it.
05
Keyboard navigation

BEFORE
Mouse to each yellow cell, one at a time.
AFTER
Arrow keys step through cited cells. Enter opens the source PDF.
WHY
Once an analyst trusts Mason's citations, they shift from "check each one carefully" to "scan and approve in a rhythm." That's the workflow that wins back time. Mousing to each yellow cell fights the rhythm. Arrow keys to step through and Enter to open the source lets them stay on the keyboard for the whole pass, which is the difference between Mason helping and being one more piece of software to drive.
